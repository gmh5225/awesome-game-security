# Headless game-asset reverse engineering, without the original toolchain

*A method, not a tool — how to get a game's meshes, animations, textures and
blendshapes into Blender or a modern engine when the original editor/SDK is gone,
closed, or unusable. Written from the asset pipeline behind the AE1 engine, across
three very different games' formats.*

This is a devlog about **process**. It contains no extracted data, no proprietary
format dumps, and no game files — just the working method and the habits that make it
reliable. Point your own tools at files **you legally own**.

---

## The situation

You want a model/animation/texture out of a game and into Blender, three.js, Unreal —
somewhere you can actually use it. But the official exporter never existed, or it's a
closed plugin for a DCC you don't run, or it's a decade dead. So you decode the files
yourself. Done carelessly this is an endless swamp of "it's *almost* right": the mesh
loads but lies on its side; the character animates but her face melts; the texture
reads but the foliage is black.

The method below is what turned that swamp into a repeatable pipeline.

## 1. Find the maintained reference first — don't decode blind

Before writing a byte of decoder, go find the people who already mapped the format.
Almost every game engine with a modding scene has a **reference implementation** or
format notes maintained by someone who's been at it for years. Read it. Transcribe the
tricky parts **verbatim** — the keyframe codec table, the quantization constants, the
chunk layout — and **cite it** in your code. A codec you reimplement "from
understanding" will be subtly wrong in the one branch you didn't hit; a codec you
transcribe from a maintained reference is wrong only where the reference is.

> The RE Engine work leaned on a maintained community library's decoders; the OSRS
> cache work stood on the shoulders of the archival/runtime-client community. In both
> cases the fastest path to correct was "read the reference, transcribe exactly, then
> verify," not "reverse it from scratch."

Finding the reference is itself a skill: search the format's magic bytes and extension,
the game engine's name, "modding"/"ripping"/"research," and the repos of the people
whose names keep appearing. Budget an hour for this before you touch a hex editor. It
pays for itself ten times over.

## 2. Fix the decode — no hacks, no band-aids

When the output is wrong, the **decode** is wrong. Resist the urge to paper over it
downstream — flipping a sign in the renderer, scaling by a magic 0.01, nudging a bone
in Blender. Those "fixes" accumulate into a pipeline nobody can reason about, and they
break the moment you touch a different asset.

Find the real cause. "The character's face distorts" turned out to be a **keyframe
codec** branch decoded with the wrong axis order and bit width — not a skinning or
retarget bug, which is where everyone first looks. "Foliage mips to black" was the
**transparent texels keyed to black RGB** bleeding under mipmapping — a texture-data
problem, fixed in the data (edge-pad the colour), not a shader hack. Fixing the
decode once fixes every asset; a band-aid fixes one and lies about the rest.

## 3. Judge with ground truth — and bisect visually

You cannot eyeball a 4×4 matrix or a packed-float dequant and know it's right. You
need a **reference** and a **render**.

- **Reference render.** Get a known-good image of the asset — an in-game screenshot,
  the game's own model viewer, the reference library's output. That's your oracle.
- **Headless render loop.** Script Blender (`blender --background --python
  render.py`) or your engine to load the decoded asset and render a PNG from a fixed
  camera/light. Read the PNG, compare to the oracle, iterate. No GUI, no clicking —
  the render *is* the test, and it runs in CI.
- **Bisect the image, not just the code.** Two techniques isolate "which piece is
  wrong" faster than any debugger:
  - **Flat-colour** every material. Strips lighting/texture noise so you see pure
    geometry/orientation — is the *shape* right?
  - **Hide one mesh** (or show only one). The eye you thought was rolled back was an
    untextured **cornea rendering opaque**; hiding shells one at a time found it in
    minutes. When something's wrong, the fastest question is "which submesh?"

These three — oracle, headless render, visual bisection — are the whole QA loop. Trust
the render over your mental model of the math every time.

## 4. Report honest status

"Diana: 13 animation sets baked, faces correct, hair cutouts clean. 2B: locomotion
works, one driver clip stalls — deferred." That's a status. "Done" when it's "mostly"
costs you (and anyone building on it) far more later than the honest line costs now.
Keep a per-session findings log: what you proved, what you measured, what's still
wrong, and the exact repro. Future-you reads it before future-you re-derives it.

## 5. IP discipline — ship code, never assets

Everything you extract is someone else's copyrighted content. Keep it **out of version
control** (a `.gitignore` for the bytes, the caches, the renders) and out of releases.
Your *tools* are yours to share — decoders point at the user's own legally-owned files,
exactly like the maintained references you learned from operate. The rule that keeps
this clean: **release code and knowledge, distribute no assets**, and when in doubt
look at how the established community tools draw that line.

## The headless toolchain, in one breath

Dependency-light Python decoders (parse the container, honour the reference's codec
tables) → a glTF/Blender bridge → a headless render-to-PNG oracle check → a findings
log → assets gitignored. No original SDK, no DCC clicking, every step scriptable and
verifiable.

## Why "headless" matters

A GUI pipeline is a pipeline only you can run, once, by hand. A **headless** one — pure
scripts from file-in to render-out — is a pipeline that runs in CI, bisects itself, and
tells the truth on every asset, not just the one you happened to open. That's the
difference between "I got it working" and "it works."
